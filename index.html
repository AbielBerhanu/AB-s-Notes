1. Kinematics: The Study of Motion

Welcome to the first chapter of Physics 1! Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that describes the motion of objects without considering the forces that cause the motion. We'll start with the fundamental concepts that form the building blocks for everything else we'll study this semester. We'll focus on motion in one dimension first, then expand our understanding to two dimensions.

1.1 Position, Displacement, and Distance

To describe motion, we first need to be able to describe an object's position. In one dimension, an object's position is its location on a number line, often denoted by the variable $x$ or $y$.

1.2 Velocity and Speed

Average velocity is the displacement divided by the time interval. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at a specific moment in time, the derivative of position with respect to time: $ v = dx/dt $.

1.3 Acceleration

Acceleration describes how the velocity of an object is changing. It is the derivative of velocity with respect to time: $ a = dv/dt $.

1.4 Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration

For motion with constant acceleration, a set of four powerful equations can be used to relate displacement, velocity, acceleration, and time.

  • $v_f = v_i + at$
  • $\Delta x = v_i t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$
  • $v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2a\Delta x$
  • $\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)t$

2. Newton's Laws of Motion

Sir Isaac Newton formulated three fundamental laws of motion that are the foundation of classical mechanics. These laws describe the relationship between an object and the forces acting upon it.

2.1 Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

An object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.

2.2 Newton's Second Law: Force and Acceleration

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass: $ \Sigma \vec{F} = m\vec{a} $.

2.3 Newton's Third Law: Action-Reaction

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.

3. Work and Energy

The concepts of work and energy provide a powerful alternative framework for analyzing motion, often simplifying complex problems.

3.1 Work and Kinetic Energy

Work is done when a force causes displacement ($ W = Fd\cos\theta $). The Work-Energy Theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy ($ K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 $).

3.2 Potential Energy and Conservation

Potential Energy ($U$) is stored energy. The total mechanical energy ($E = K + U$) of an isolated system is conserved if only conservative forces (like gravity) do work.

4. Linear Momentum and Collisions

Momentum, or "mass in motion," is a crucial concept for understanding collisions and interactions between objects.

4.1 Linear Momentum and Impulse

Linear momentum ($\vec{p}$) is a vector defined as $ \vec{p} = m\vec{v} $. Impulse ($\vec{J}$) is the change in momentum, equal to the net force applied over a time interval: $ \vec{J} = \Delta\vec{p} = \vec{F}_{net}\Delta t $.

4.2 Conservation of Momentum

For an isolated system (no external net forces), the total momentum is constant. This is powerful for analyzing collisions.

4.3 Collisions

In elastic collisions, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In inelastic collisions, momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not.

5. Rotational Motion

We now consider the motion of extended, rigid bodies as they rotate about an axis.

5.1 Rotational Kinematics & Dynamics

We use angular variables: angular position ($\theta$), velocity ($\omega$), and acceleration ($\alpha$). Torque ($\tau$) is the rotational equivalent of force, and Moment of Inertia ($I$) is the rotational equivalent of mass. Newton's Second Law for rotation is: $ \Sigma \vec{\tau} = I\vec{\alpha} $.

6. Oscillations & Waves

This final section covers periodic motion—motion that repeats itself in a regular cycle—and the propagation of disturbances, known as waves.

6.1 Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

SHM is periodic motion where the restoring force is proportional to the displacement (e.g., a mass on a spring, $F = -kx$). The period ($T$) is the time for one full oscillation.

6.2 Waves

A wave is a disturbance transferring energy. Key properties are amplitude, wavelength ($\lambda$), frequency ($f$), and speed ($v = f\lambda$).
