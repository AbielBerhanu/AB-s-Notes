Unit 1: Units, Measurement, and Vectors

Key Formulas

  • $|\mathbf{R}| = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2}$
    • - Magnitude of a resultant vector $\mathbf{R}$ from perpendicular components $R_x$ and $R_y$.
  • $\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{R_y}{R_x}\right)$
    • - Direction $\theta$ of a resultant vector relative to the x-axis.

Overview

This introductory unit covers the foundation of scientific measurement and vector quantities. We begin with the SI units of length (meter), mass (kilogram), and time (second). We also discuss measurement uncertainty and significant figures. The second part introduces vectors and scalars. Scalars have magnitude only (e.g. distance or speed), whereas vectors have both magnitude and direction. We learn how to represent vectors graphically (with arrows) and numerically (via components), and how to add and subtract them.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 1

Example 1: Unit Conversion

A car is traveling at 60.0 miles per hour (mi/h). Convert this speed to meters per second (m/s).

Solution: Use conversion factors: 1 mile = 1609 m, and 1 hour = 3600 s. Set up the conversion so units cancel appropriately:

$$ 60.0~\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1609~\text{m}}{1~\text{mi}} \times \frac{1~\text{h}}{3600~\text{s}} = \frac{60.0 \times 1609}{3600}~\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \approx 26.8~\text{m/s} $$

Answer: 60.0 mi/h is approximately 26.8 m/s.

Example 2: Vector Addition

Suppose you walk 3.0 km due East, then 4.0 km due North. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant displacement $\mathbf{R}$.

Solution: The two displacements are perpendicular vectors. Use the Pythagorean theorem for the magnitude:

$$ |\mathbf{R}| = \sqrt{(3.0~\text{km})^2 + (4.0~\text{km})^2} = 5.0~\text{km} $$

Find the direction using the tangent function:

$$ \tan\theta = \frac{4.0~\text{km}}{3.0~\text{km}} \implies \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{4.0}{3.0}\right) \approx 53^\circ $$

Answer: The resultant displacement is 5.0 km at 53° North of East.

Unit 2: One-Dimensional Kinematics

Key Formulas (for constant acceleration)

  • $v = v_0 + at$
    • - $v$: final velocity
    • - $v_0$: initial velocity
    • - $a$: acceleration
    • - $t$: time interval
  • $x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2$
    • - $x$: final position
    • - $x_0$: initial position
  • $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)$
    • - Relates variables without time.

Overview

Kinematics is the study of motion without regard to the forces that cause it. In one dimension (1D), we describe motion along a straight line. Key concepts are displacement ($\Delta x$), velocity ($v$), and acceleration ($a$). Accelerated motion with constant acceleration is described by a set of key equations. Free-fall under gravity is a common 1D case where acceleration $a = -g \approx -9.8~\text{m/s}^2$.

Example 1: Uniform Acceleration

A car initially at rest accelerates uniformly at $3.0~\text{m/s}^2$. (a) How fast will it be moving after 5.0 s? (b) How far will it have traveled?

Solution (a): Use $v = v_0 + at$. With $v_0=0$: $v = (3.0~\text{m/s}^2)(5.0~\text{s}) = 15.0~\text{m/s}$.

Solution (b): Use $x = v_0t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$. With $v_0=0$: $x = \frac{1}{2}(3.0~\text{m/s}^2)(5.0~\text{s})^2 = 37.5~\text{m}$.

Answer: (a) 15.0 m/s, (b) 37.5 m.

Example 2: Free Fall

A ball is thrown straight up with an initial speed of 20 m/s. (a) How high does it rise? (b) How long does it take to return to the release level?

Solution (a): At the highest point, $v=0$. Use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta y$. Let $a=-9.8~\text{m/s}^2$.

$$ 0 = (20)^2 + 2(-9.8)\Delta y \implies \Delta y = \frac{-400}{-19.6} \approx 20.4~\text{m} $$

Solution (b): When the ball returns, displacement $\Delta y = 0$. Use $y = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}a t^2$.

$$ 0 = 20t - 4.9t^2 = t(20 - 4.9t) \implies t = \frac{20}{4.9} \approx 4.1~\text{s} $$

Answer: Max height ≈ 20.4 m, total time ≈ 4.1 s.

Unit 3: Two-Dimensional Motion (Projectile Motion)

Key Formulas

  • $v_{ix} = v_i \cos\theta \quad | \quad v_{iy} = v_i \sin\theta$
    • - $v_i$: initial launch speed
    • - $\theta$: launch angle
    • - $v_{ix}, v_{iy}$: initial x and y components of velocity
  • $x(t) = v_{ix}t$
    • - Horizontal position at time $t$.
  • $y(t) = v_{iy}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$
    • - Vertical position at time $t$.
    • - $g$: acceleration due to gravity (~9.8 m/s²)

Overview

This unit extends kinematics to two dimensions (2D). A key example is projectile motion. The critical concept is that horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The horizontal motion has constant velocity ($a_x=0$), while the vertical motion has constant downward acceleration ($a_y=-g$). The combination produces a parabolic trajectory.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 4

Interactive Projectile Simulator

Example 1: Projectile Launched at an Angle

A soccer ball is kicked from ground level at 25 m/s at a 30° angle. (a) How long is it in the air? (b) What is its range?

Solution: Initial velocity components: $v_{0x} = 25\cos30^\circ \approx 21.7~\text{m/s}$ and $v_{0y} = 25\sin30^\circ = 12.5~\text{m/s}$.

(a) Time of flight: Solve for time when vertical displacement is zero: $0 = v_{0y}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.

$$ t = \frac{2v_{0y}}{g} = \frac{2(12.5)}{9.8} \approx 2.55~\text{s} $$

(b) Range: Horizontal distance $x = v_{0x} \times t = 21.7~\text{m/s} \times 2.55~\text{s} \approx 55.3~\text{m}$.

Answer: Time ≈ 2.55 s, Range ≈ 55.3 m.

Unit 4: Newton’s Laws of Motion

Key Formulas

  • $\sum \mathbf{F} = m \mathbf{a}$
    • - $\sum \mathbf{F}$: The net (vector sum) force on an object.
    • - $m$: mass of the object.
    • - $\mathbf{a}$: acceleration of the object.

Overview

This unit introduces Newton’s three laws, which form the basis for dynamics. The first law defines inertia. The second law quantifies the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration as $\sum \mathbf{F} = m \mathbf{a}$. The third law states that forces come in equal and opposite action-reaction pairs. We use free-body diagrams to identify all forces (weight, normal force, tension, friction) on an object.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 6

Example: Net Force and Acceleration

A 5.0 kg box is subjected to two forces: $F_1 = 20$ N East, and $F_2 = 15$ N North. Find the box’s acceleration.

Solution: Find the net force using vector addition. The magnitude is:

$$ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{(20~\text{N})^2 + (15~\text{N})^2} = 25~\text{N} $$

Apply Newton's Second Law to find acceleration:

$$ a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{25~\text{N}}{5.0~\text{kg}} = 5.0~\text{m/s}^2 $$

The direction is $\theta = \arctan(15/20) \approx 36.9^\circ$ North of East.

Answer: a = 5.0 m/s² at 36.9° North of East.

Unit 5: Applications of Newton’s Laws

Key Formulas

  • $f_s \le \mu_s N$
    • - $f_s$: force of static friction
    • - $\mu_s$: coefficient of static friction
    • - $N$: normal force
  • $f_k = \mu_k N$
    • - $f_k$: force of kinetic friction
    • - $\mu_k$: coefficient of kinetic friction
  • $F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}$
    • - $F_c$: centripetal force
    • - $v$: tangential speed
    • - $r$: radius of circular path

Overview

We apply Newton’s laws to more complex situations involving friction, tension, and inclined planes. Friction opposes motion, with static friction ($f_s$) preventing motion and kinetic friction ($f_k$) opposing it. Tension is a pulling force through a rope. For objects on an incline, we resolve weight into components parallel and perpendicular to the slope.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 8

Interactive: Block on an Incline

Example: Atwood’s Machine

Two masses, $m_1 = 4.0$ kg and $m_2 = 6.0$ kg, are connected by a light rope over a frictionless pulley. Find their acceleration.

Solution: The net force on the system is the difference in weights: $F_{net} = (m_2 - m_1)g$. This net force accelerates the total mass $M = m_1 + m_2$.

$$ a = \frac{F_{net}}{M} = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{(6.0 - 4.0)(9.8)}{6.0 + 4.0} = \frac{19.6}{10} = 1.96~\text{m/s}^2 $$

Answer: The acceleration is 1.96 m/s².

Unit 6: Work and Energy

Key Formulas

  • $W = Fd\cos\theta$
    • - $W$: work done by a constant force
    • - $F$: magnitude of the force
    • - $d$: magnitude of the displacement
    • - $\theta$: angle between force and displacement
  • $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
    • - $KE$: kinetic energy
    • - $m$: mass, $v$: speed
  • $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$
    • - Work-Energy Theorem: Net work equals change in kinetic energy.

Overview

This unit introduces the concepts of work, kinetic energy, and the work-energy principle. In physics, work $W$ is defined as the energy transferred by a force acting over a displacement. For a constant force $F$ acting on an object that undergoes displacement $d$, the work done by that force is $W = Fd\cos\theta$. Kinetic Energy (KE) is the energy of motion: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. The Work-Energy Theorem states that the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy, $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 11

Example: Work Done by a Force at an Angle

A person pulls a sled along level ground by applying a 100 N force via a rope that makes a 30° angle above the horizontal. The sled moves 10 m forward. How much work is done?

Solution: Use the formula $W = Fd\cos\theta$.

$$ W = (100~\text{N})(10~\text{m})\cos(30^\circ) \approx 866~\text{J} $$

Answer: 866 J of work is done by the person on the sled.

Unit 7: Conservation of Energy

Key Formulas

  • $U_g = mgh$
    • - $U_g$: gravitational potential energy
    • - $h$: height above a reference level
  • $U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$
    • - $U_s$: elastic (spring) potential energy
    • - $k$: spring constant, $x$: displacement from equilibrium
  • $KE_i + U_i = KE_f + U_f$
    • - Conservation of Mechanical Energy (no non-conservative forces).

Overview

This unit formalizes potential energy (PE) and the principle of energy conservation. For conservative forces (like gravity or springs), we can define a potential energy function $U$. The Conservation of Mechanical Energy states that in the absence of non-conservative work (like friction), the total mechanical energy $E = KE + PE$ of a system remains constant.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – Gravity, Pendulums, and Conservation of Energy

Interactive: Roller Coaster Energy

Example: Converting Spring PE to Gravitational PE

A spring ($k=500$ N/m) is compressed by 0.20 m with a 1.5 kg ball. When released, how high does the ball go?

Solution: Initial energy is all spring potential: $U_{s,i} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(500)(0.20)^2 = 10$ J. At max height, all energy is gravitational potential: $PE_f = mgh_{max}$.

$$ PE_f = U_{s,i} \implies mgh_{max} = 10~\text{J} $$ $$ h_{max} = \frac{10}{(1.5)(9.8)} \approx 0.68~\text{m} $$

Answer: The ball rises about 0.68 m.

Unit 8: Momentum and Impulse

Key Formulas

  • $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$
    • - $\mathbf{p}$: linear momentum
  • $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{F}_{avg}\Delta t$
    • - $\mathbf{J}$: impulse
  • $\mathbf{J} = \Delta\mathbf{p}$
    • - Impulse-Momentum Theorem

Overview

Linear momentum $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$ is "quantity of motion." An impulse $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{F}_{avg}\Delta t$ equals the change in momentum ($\mathbf{J} = \Delta\mathbf{p}$). This is useful for analyzing situations with large forces acting for short times, like collisions.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 15

Example: Impulse on a bouncing ball

A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and bounces back at 8 m/s. What was the average force if contact lasted 0.05 s?

Solution: Let the initial direction be positive. $v_i = +10$ m/s, $v_f = -8$ m/s.

$$ \Delta p = mv_f - mv_i = 0.20(-8) - 0.20(10) = -3.6~\text{kg·m/s} $$ $$ F_{avg} = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{-3.6}{0.05} = -72~\text{N} $$

Answer: The average force was 72 N, directed away from the wall.

Unit 9: Collisions

Key Formulas

  • $\sum \mathbf{p}_i = \sum \mathbf{p}_f$
    • - Conservation of Momentum (always applies in isolated systems).
  • $\sum KE_i = \sum KE_f$
    • - Conservation of Kinetic Energy (only applies for elastic collisions).

Overview

This unit applies momentum conservation to collisions. In any collision within an isolated system, total momentum is conserved. We distinguish between elastic collisions (where kinetic energy is also conserved) and inelastic collisions (where it is not). In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together.

Unit 10: Rotational Kinematics

Key Formulas (for constant angular acceleration)

  • $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$
    • - $\omega$: final angular velocity
    • - $\omega_0$: initial angular velocity
    • - $\alpha$: angular acceleration
  • $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$
    • - $\theta$: final angular position
    • - $\theta_0$: initial angular position
  • $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha (\theta - \theta_0)$

Overview

Rotational kinematics describes angular motion. Key quantities are angular displacement ($\theta$), angular velocity ($\omega$), and angular acceleration ($\alpha$). The kinematic equations for constant angular acceleration mirror those for linear motion.

Video Lecture: Walter Lewin – MIT 8.01x Lecture 19

Example: Spinning up a wheel

A turntable at rest accelerates uniformly with $\alpha = 2.0~\text{rad/s}^2$. (a) How long to reach $\omega = 6.0$ rad/s? (b) What angle does it rotate in that time?

Solution (a): Use $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$. $6.0 = 0 + 2.0t \implies t = 3.0$ s.

Solution (b): Use $\theta = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$. $\theta = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(2.0)(3.0)^2 = 9.0$ rad.

Answer: (a) 3.0 s, (b) 9.0 radians.

Unit 11: Rotational Dynamics

Key Formulas

  • $\tau = rF\sin\phi$
    • - $\tau$: torque, $r$: lever arm, $F$: force, $\phi$: angle
  • $\sum \tau = I \alpha$
    • - Newton's 2nd Law for Rotation ($I$: moment of inertia)
  • $L = I\omega$
    • - $L$: angular momentum
  • $L_i = L_f$
    • - Conservation of Angular Momentum

Overview

This unit connects forces to rotational motion. Torque ($\tau$) is the rotational equivalent of force. Moment of Inertia ($I$) is the rotational equivalent of mass. Newton’s second law for rotation is $\sum \tau = I \alpha$. Angular momentum, $L=I\omega$, is conserved if net external torque is zero.
